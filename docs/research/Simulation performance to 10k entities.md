# Flatten tiles, cache sight, then stagger senses

Bluproto can reach 10,000 bodies inside its 30 ms tick on the same 4-core box, but no single technique gets it there: the arithmetic demands a **~5x reduction in per-body cost on top of a 10x rise in body count**, and the survey of shipped engines and agent-simulation literature shows that every system at that scale attacks the problem in layers. The ordering that the evidence supports is: first the exact optimisations that leave every scenario fixture bit-identical (a flat primitive tile grid in place of `Tile[][]`, an exact integer grid traversal in place of the 8-samples-per-tile march, one LOS evaluation per unordered pair, and allocation-free sense buffers), which together should take the ray march from ~46% of the tick to a minor line item; then a tile-pair visibility cache that makes each LOS test an O(1) bit test but requires quantising sight to tile centres; then a parallel sense phase over a frozen snapshot with an id-sorted apply, which local measurements put at **3.3-3.6x on this hardware** for the sense work but which changes the tick from sequential to synchronous update; and finally the behaviour-changing schedulers that every 10k-agent game relies on (Factorio-style sleep, RimWorld-style hash-offset cadences, Unreal-style per-tick ray budgets), which bound the tick regardless of population at the price of bounded reaction latency. The critical unknown is the cost of the *act* phase: at 10,000 bodies a serial act of 3 µs per body is 30 ms on its own, so whether steps one to four suffice, or whether the schedulers become mandatory, is decided by a profile that has not yet been taken. Each step below is labelled exact or behaviour-changing, and the repo's own rule applies to every behaviour-changing one: judge it on the demo world's guild counts, not on a passing scenario.

## Ten thousand bodies means 160,000 rays, and the march cannot survive that

The profile fixes the shape of the problem. Today ~16,000 rays per tick at ~350 ns each cost ~5.6 ms, most of a sense pass that is 46% of a ~15 ms tick at 1,000 bodies. The uniform-grid index keeps the number of candidate neighbours per body constant at constant density, so scaling to 10,000 bodies multiplies everything by ten: **~160,000 rays at ~56 ms**, nearly twice the whole budget before a single body thinks or moves, inside a projected ~150 ms tick. The per-body wall-clock budget at the target is 3 µs against ~15 µs today. That is why the plan has to start with the ray, and why the ray alone is not enough.

The march is expensive for a structural reason rather than a tuning one. `Grid.hasLOS` samples `ceil(len*8)` points along the segment and calls `isLosConnected` on every tile change; each of those calls resolves `tiles[c2][r2]` through a `Tile[][]` of 50,688 separately allocated objects, so every tile touched is at least two dependent pointer loads before a field is read. On HotSpot each of those objects carries a 12-byte header plus 8-byte alignment and is reached through a 4-byte compressed reference ([openjdk/jol README](https://github.com/openjdk/jol/blob/master/README.md)), which spreads a 50 KB grid's worth of information across megabytes of heap, and a cache miss costs on the order of 200 cycles ([Mike Acton, CppCon 2014 via isocpp.org](https://isocpp.org/blog/2015/01/cppcon-2014-data-oriented-design-and-c-mike-acton)). Before the march even starts, line 1615 evaluates a `Math.atan2` per candidate for the cone test, which is both a cost and a replay hazard: the `Math` Javadoc states that its implementations "are not defined to return the bit-for-bit same results" and encourages platform intrinsics ([openjdk Math.java](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/openjdk/jdk/master/src/java.base/share/classes/java/lang/Math.java)).

The comparables confirm that bluproto is not language-limited but LOS-limited. In the JuliaDynamics cross-framework benchmark, MASON (Java) runs the large flocking model at **0.61x the time of Agents.jl**, faster than the Julia reference, and the standard ABM benchmark ladder runs Flockers and Wolf-Sheep-Grass from 1,000 to 128,000 agents at 10% density on a single i7 ([ABM_Framework_Comparisons](https://github.com/JuliaDynamics/ABM_Framework_Comparisons); [isislab-unisa/ABM_Comparison](https://github.com/isislab-unisa/ABM_Comparison)). None of those frameworks casts an occluded ray per neighbour pair; their sensing is a radius query on a discretised field. Iteration itself is not the cost either: Dominion, the fastest Java ECS measured, sweeps 10 million entities in **0.7-3.9 ns per entity per pass** ([Dominion benchmarks](https://github.com/dominion-dev/dominion-ecs-java/blob/main/dominion-ecs-engine-benchmarks/README.md)), and Fleks steps 20,000 entities in roughly 33 µs ([Quillraven/Fleks](https://github.com/Quillraven/Fleks)), so a full 10,000-body pass is tens of microseconds if the per-body work is trivial. The one shipped simulation whose design matches bluproto's is the cautionary one: Dwarf Fortress, where creature-to-creature line-of-sight is reported at roughly 20% of CPU even after a 26-tile cutoff, and whose eventual answer was to multithread the LOS loop ([Steam: LOS improvements in 50.05, search snippet](https://steamcommunity.com/app/975370/discussions/0/3761101693151034404/)).

## Exact wins first: flatten the grid, traverse exactly, evaluate each pair once

Three changes leave every answer identical and together should remove most of the ray cost. The first is the tile representation: split the hot per-tile properties out of `Tile` into primitive arrays indexed by `c*rows + r`, so the ray loop reads one `byte[]` (or one `long[]` bitset of 6.3 KB) for opacity and one for the corner-cut connectivity rule, and nothing else. The whole 288x176 grid as a `byte[]` is ~50 KB and stays resident in L1/L2 for the entire tick, versus two dependent and frequently missing loads per tile today. This is a re-encoding of the same values with the same operations, so it is determinism-neutral by construction; cold tile data can stay as objects in a side table keyed by tile index. No Java benchmark with a clean "tile objects vs `byte[]`" multiplier was reachable, so the expected gain is arithmetic rather than citation: at ~5-20 tile touches per ray and 80k-320k touches per tick, every 100k misses avoided is worth ~5-7 ms on a 3-4 GHz core, which is consistent with the profile placing tile lookups at the top.

The second is the traversal. Amanatides and Woo's grid traversal moves from one cell to its neighbour with "only two floating point comparisons and one floating point addition" and visits exactly the cells a segment enters ([Amanatides & Woo abstract, via voxel-wiki](https://github.com/voxel-wiki/voxel-wiki.github.io/blob/main/content/wiki/raytracing/index.md); reference implementation at [fast_voxel_traversal](https://github.com/francisengelmann/fast_voxel_traversal)). A segment of length L crosses between L and 1.41L tile boundaries, so the loop drops from 8 iterations per tile of length to roughly 1-1.4, a **5.7-8x cut in loop iterations**, while `isLosConnected` runs the same number of times as before. Done in integer fixed point (tile centres at 2x+1, 2y+1) the walk is exact, needs no `Math.sqrt` or `ceil`, and, with a fixed tie rule for a corner exactly on the line, is symmetric. One caveat deserves a line in the commit: the current sampler can skip a tile whose in-segment length is under 1/8 tile and can emit a diagonal step where the true segment crosses two orthogonal neighbours, so the exact traversal is exact for the geometry but not bit-identical to today's answers at corner clips. A few scenario fixtures may need re-baselining, and any that do are ones where the old march was wrong. The cone test should go the same way: replace the per-candidate `atan2` with a dot-product against the observer's precomputed heading vector and `cos(fov)`, computed once per body per tick, which is geometrically equivalent, removes the last transcendental from the inner loop, and, if the per-body values come from `StrictMath`, makes the result reproducible across JIT tiers and machines, the fix one lockstep engine reached by vendoring fdlibm after last-bit `sin`/`atan2` differences produced desyncs ([supcom-engine PR #33](https://github.com/bwiemz/supcom-engine/pull/33)).

The third is symmetry. With an exact, symmetric traversal, the geometric part of LOS(a,b) equals LOS(b,a), which RogueBasin's strict centre-to-centre definition already exploits: "The fact it is perfectly symetric also enable re-use of computations; monsters that are lit by the player can be considered as seeing the player without los check" ([RogueBasin: LOS using strict definition](https://github.com/Chizaruu/roguebasin/blob/main/wiki/los_using_strict_definition.md)). The 2009 libtcod study measured the asymmetry of common per-observer algorithms at 0.75-6.9% of visible cells outdoors, so a symmetric traversal is also a correctness improvement ([Jice, comparative FOV study](https://github.com/Chizaruu/roguebasin/blob/main/wiki/comparative_study_of_field_of_view_algorithms_for_2d_grid_based_worlds.md)). Because A's segment to B and B's segment to A are the same segment even with sub-tile endpoints, pair sharing does not require quantising to tiles: march once per unordered pair, then apply each observer's range and cone per direction. The saving is up to 50% of rays, realised only for pairs where both sense ranges (3-24 tiles) cover the distance.

Alongside these, the sense pass should become allocation-free: per-body output slots in parallel primitive arrays, a counting-sort rebuild of the uniform grid into pre-sized `int[]` cell-start/entity arrays in stable id order, and no per-level list rebuilds. HotSpot's escape analysis scalar-replaces objects in only about 13% of candidate methods and gives up at any non-inlined callee or control-flow merge ([HotSpot escape analysis study, mirror](https://gist.github.com/JohnTortugo/c2607821202634a6509ec3c321ebf370)), and C2 inlines freely only under 35 bytecodes and hot methods under 325 ([JVM optimisation tips](https://gist.github.com/djspiewak/464c11307cabc80171c90397d4ec34ef)), so per-tick garbage has to be removed by hand and the ray kernel kept small and monomorphic. This step is exact, is a prerequisite for the parallel phase (which needs one writer per slot and no shared allocation), and its own gain should be measured rather than assumed; the ECS numbers above say iteration overhead is not where the time goes, so the payoff is in cache misses and GC pauses. Valhalla and compact object headers are not options on Java 21: JEP 401 is a preview in JDK 28 and JEP 519's headers are JDK 25 ([JEP 401, search snippet](https://openjdk.org/jeps/401); [JEP 519, search snippet](https://openjdk.org/jeps/519)).

## A tile-pair visibility table makes LOS O(1), at the price of tile-centre semantics

If after step one the ray is still a top cost at 10,000 bodies, the next lever is to stop marching at query time. On a static opacity map, visibility between two tiles is a pure function of the map, and a table of it is the only structure that makes the per-pair cost O(1). Sizing for bluproto: a radius-24 window is 49x49 = 2,401 bits per tile, **~15 MB for the full table or ~7.5 MB with symmetric halving**, filled once at load by ~45 million rays (roughly 16 s single-threaded at today's per-ray cost, seconds after step one, and trivially parallel because it is pure). FastLOS is the same idea compressed: "it can calculate Line of Sight between any two squares in constant time" with a distance check and a bitmask AND, conservative and symmetric, at the cost of a heavy preprocessing stage on cave-like maps ([RogueBasin: FastLOS](https://github.com/Chizaruu/roguebasin/blob/main/wiki/fastlos.md)). The really-symmetric visibility trie is the per-observer alternative, measured at **5.7-8x faster than per-cell rays at radius 20** with zero one-way failures over 747,870 tested paths, but it costs "significantly more RAM" at large radii and is built once per level ([denismr/SymmetricPCVT](https://github.com/denismr/SymmetricPCVT/blob/master/README.md)).

Two honest caveats decide whether this step is exact. The first is that `hasLOS` takes sub-tile double positions today, and a table is indexed by tile; quantising sight to tile centres is the strict definition RogueBasin calls "exact (no artifact or asymmetry)" but it is a change to what a body can see at a wall corner, so this step is a **small behaviour change**, not an exact optimisation. The second is dynamic occluders. `isLosConnected` treats tall-grass cover as opaque alongside walls, and vegetation grows and is eaten, so the map is not static. A change to one opaque tile invalidates every entry whose ray crosses it; the cheap conservative rule recomputes the FOV of the ~1,800 tiles within radius 24 of the change, 0.05-0.7 s per event, which is affordable only if opacity changes are rare or batched. The workable design is therefore layered: a precomputed table over the static occluders (walls) as a hard "definitely blocked" pre-filter, with the flat exact DDA of step one run only for pairs the static table admits, marching only against the dynamic layer. That keeps every answer exact for a fixed tile-centre definition, is deterministic as long as any lazy fill is keyed on simulation state rather than wall-clock budgets, and can be promoted to a full cached table later if profiling shows tall-grass churn is low. A per-body footprint cache (recompute a body's FOV only when it changes tile, as the RTS engines do) is the alternative for observers with short radii, where the "efficient observation" analysis shows a one-tile move changes only 11+11 cells out of 98 at radius 5 ([RogueBasin: An Efficient Observation Algorithm](https://github.com/Chizaruu/roguebasin/blob/main/wiki/an_efficient_observation_algorithm.md)); for radius-24 observers a full shadowcast is ~1,800 cells and loses to the table.

Per-observer FOV as a replacement for per-pair rays should not be the default. Recursive shadowcasting was the fastest general method in the only comparative benchmark found, but on 2009 hardware it still cost 32 µs per FOV on a 40x40 indoor map ([Jice, comparative FOV study](https://github.com/Chizaruu/roguebasin/blob/main/wiki/comparative_study_of_field_of_view_algorithms_for_2d_grid_based_worlds.md)); 10,000 of those per tick is 320 ms. It wins only for short-radius observers with many neighbours, so whether it is worth a code path at all depends on the distribution of sense ranges, which the profiler can report.

## Four cores buy about 2.3x overall, only if sense reads a frozen snapshot

Every production engine that parallelises a deterministic simulation does it the same way: a parallel read-only phase over frozen state, then an ordered apply. Unity's entity command buffers record a sort key per command because "the order of commands recorded from different threads depends upon thread scheduling", and playback sorts by that key so the result is deterministic ([Unity ECS samples: entity command buffers](https://github.com/Unity-Technologies/EntityComponentSystemSamples/blob/master/EntitiesSamples/Docs/entity-command-buffers.md)). Factorio's belt threading has worker threads add wake-up requests to a list that the main thread merges "in a deterministic way based on the group update order number" ([Factorio FFF-364, search snippet](https://factorio.com/blog/post/fff-364)). For bluproto the mapping is direct: each body's sense and think write only into their own pre-allocated output slots, the RNG is advanced only in the serial phase or split per body deterministically, and the act phase walks bodies in id order exactly as today.

The local measurements put numbers on it for this box (4 x Xeon 2.10 GHz, JDK 21, non-JMH, treat as ±20%). With per-body work of ~0.2-2 µs, **four fixed id-range partitions via `ForkJoinTask.invokeAll` reach 3.3-3.6x at both 1,000 and 10,000 bodies**, per-element `IntStream.parallel()` reaches 3.1x at ~2 µs per body but is slower than sequential below ~0.2 µs per body, empty fan-out of four tasks costs ~2 µs, and false sharing on adjacent volatile stores costs 2.6x ([local harness, scratchpad FjBench.java](file:///tmp/claude-0/-home-user-bluproto/0be94016-96e2-53df-94b3-6fde065aaab2/scratchpad/FjBench.java)). Doug Lea's guidance agrees: break-even for parallel streams is near N x Q ≈ 10,000 element-operations, and the worst slowdowns come from waking threads that have nothing to do ([Stream Parallel Guidance, search snippet](https://gee.cs.oswego.edu/dl/html/StreamParallelGuidance.html)). Bluproto's ~15 µs per body is far above the threshold, so dispatch overhead is under 5% and the limits are Amdahl and memory bandwidth. Use a dedicated `ForkJoinPool(4)` owned by the runner rather than the common pool (parallelism 3 here, shared with everything else, and parked between ticks), fixed partitions rather than work stealing, and per-partition reductions merged in partition order.

The ceiling is Amdahl's. If sense plus think are 80% of the tick and the parallel part scales 3.5x, the whole tick speeds up 1/(0.2 + 0.8/3.5) ≈ **2.3x**: 15 ms becomes ~6.5 ms at 1,000 bodies and ~65 ms at 10,000 before the algorithmic steps. The order therefore matters less than doing both: steps one and two shrink the parallel share, which lowers the marginal gain of step four, while the serial act share becomes the floor. BioDynaMo's 67-76x on 72 cores came only after replacing a serial kd-tree build with a parallel uniform grid ([BioDynaMo, arXiv 2301.06984, search snippet](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.06984)), so the counting-sort grid rebuild must be parallel and order-independent too. The risk to measure first is memory-boundness: Factorio's first attempt at threading belts, trains and the electric network was "actually even slower" because the threads were invalidating each other's cache ([FFF-215, search snippet](https://www.factorio.com/blog/post/fff-215)), and its later electric-network threading cut 0.5 ms to 0.39 ms at 30x the CPU use because the update "just reads two variables, does one or two additions" ([FFF-421, search snippet](https://www.factorio.com/blog/post/fff-421)). Bluproto's per-body sense is far more compute per byte than that, so it should scale like the local benchmark, but the 1/2/4-thread curve with act stubbed out should be read before the apply phase is designed.

This step preserves bit-identical replay but is a **behaviour change if sense and act are interleaved per body today**: every body would see start-of-tick state rather than the moves of lower-id bodies, which is the difference between sequential and synchronous update, and the evolutionary-game literature's canonical example shows that difference can be qualitative rather than cosmetic, with cooperation under synchronous update collapsing to all-defectors under asynchronous update ([arXiv 0911.1720, search snippet](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0911.1720)). Expect every outcome assertion in `SimTests` to need re-baselining, and run the demo world either side. Checkerboard partitioning of the act phase itself is the second parallel step, not the first: it is deterministic if colours, cell size (at least the maximum act reach) and border rules are fixed functions of state, but it imposes yet another fixed order, and given Factorio's experience it pays only if act is a large share of the tick.

## Cadence, sleep and ray budgets bound the tick, and every one is a behaviour change

Every shipped simulation at 10,000 agents stops updating everyone every tick. Factorio's entities "go to sleep when they have nothing to do, consuming zero processing time when asleep", and applying that to belts alone took factories from 25 to 35-40 UPS ([FFF-148](https://www.factorio.com/blog/post/fff-148) and [FFF-176](https://www.factorio.com/blog/post/fff-176), search snippets); a forum profile shows entity update falling from ~29 ms to ~11 ms per tick when all biters were removed, which is what autonomous units cost even in a heavily optimised C++ engine ([Factorio forums t=44246, search snippet](https://forums.factorio.com/viewtopic.php?t=44246)). RimWorld runs things at 60 Hz, every 250 ticks or every 2,000 ticks, staggered by `tick % interval == hash(thing) % interval` because a hash offset "prevents the game from stalling from many instances of the same Thing performing their work during the same tick" ([HugsLib wiki: Custom Tick Scheduling](https://github.com/UnlimitedHugs/RimworldHugsLib/wiki/Custom-Tick-Scheduling)), and its 1.6 release runs non-time-sensitive systems as slow as 4 Hz ([RimWorld 1.6 notes, search snippet](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Version/1.6.4518)). Spring's staggered LOS mode updates one fifteenth of units per frame at 30 frames per second, revisiting each every half second ([Spring LosHandler.cpp](https://github.com/spring/spring/blob/develop/rts/Sim/Misc/LosHandler.cpp)), and The Bibites desynchronised vision updates between creatures for performance, the closest artificial-life precedent ([Bibites 0.5.0 devlog, search snippet](https://thebibites.itch.io/the-bibites/devlog/464605/the-bibites-050-modernity-and-progress)).

The perception-specific template is Unreal's sight sense. It keeps one persistent query per (observer, target) pair, scores each as `Age + Importance` with importance highest inside a near-distance threshold, sorts the queue, performs at most **6 line traces per tick by default** under a 5 ms slice, runs `DistSquared <= SightRadiusSq` and a dot-product cone check before any trace, and hides latency with hysteresis (`LoseSightRadius`, `AutoSuccessRangeFromLastSeenLocation`) ([AISense_Sight.cpp, UE4 mirror](https://github.com/JimmieKJ/unrealTournament/blob/master/Engine/Source/Runtime/AIModule/Private/Perception/AISense_Sight.cpp)); UE 5.3 adds 10 async traces per tick and separate in-range and out-of-range queues walked round-robin ([AISense_Sight.cpp, UE 5.3 mirror](https://github.com/chenyong2github/UnrealEngine/blob/5.3/Engine/Source/Runtime/AIModule/Private/Perception/AISense_Sight.cpp)). The numbers are irrelevant, since a physics trace is orders of magnitude dearer than a 350 ns grid ray, but the structure imports cleanly: persistent pair records, importance ordering, pre-checks, hysteresis, and a count-based budget. Unreal Mass makes the budget explicit with a maximum entity count per LOD level ([Overview of Mass Gameplay, search snippet](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/overview-of-mass-gameplay-in-unreal-engine)), which turns a 10,000-body world into a fixed cost plus a cheap idle path; and a headless server needs no camera for it, as an interest-management implementation shows by tiering update rates purely on distance to other actors and holding **p99 tick time under 10.31 ms at 1,000 players** ([FreeMode PR #23](https://github.com/SashaGoncharov19/FreeMode/pull/23)).

For a replayable sim the rules are strict and simple. A schedule must be a pure function of (tick, body id, body state): `MaxTracesPerTick`-style counts are deterministic, `MaxTimeSlicePerTick`-style wall-clock slices are not, and the Godot proposal to stagger enemies by rendered frame is the anti-pattern, since the number of updates per physics step then depends on frame rate ([godot-proposals #10015](https://github.com/godotengine/godot-proposals/issues/10015)). Concretely: `senseDue(body) = (tick + hash(id)) % interval(tier) == 0`, with tier 0 (a predator with prey in range, or prey with a predator in range) sensing every tick, tier 1 (neighbours, no danger) every 2-4 ticks, and tier 2 (no neighbours within radius, a fact the uniform grid reports for free) asleep until a cell-entry event or a maximum of N ticks; a fixed ray budget per tick filled by importance with round-robin carry-over by id; wake events processed by cell then id. Sleeping is behaviour-neutral only if wake conditions are complete, and the dangerous failure for an ecology is a missed wake (a predator arrives and the prey does not notice until its scheduled tick), so the maximum sleep should be capped at the sense cadence. Cadence tiers add up to `interval` ticks of reaction latency, 120 ms at 4 ticks, which RimWorld and Dwarf Fortress accept for slow systems and exempt fast ones from. Under a 10/40/50 tier split with intervals 1/4/16 the average sense rate falls to ~0.23 of today's, roughly a **4x cut on the sensing share**; this is a model, not a measurement, and it is not enough on its own, which is why it comes after the exact steps rather than instead of them.

The repo's balance guidance adds a constraint no game has: if importance ranking correlates with a heritable trait (bodies with a longer sense radius get demoted less, say), the scheduler becomes a selective force. Tiers must derive from situation (neighbour count, danger, role), tie-break by id, and the change must be judged on guild counts in the demo world either side of it. Field-based sensing is the last and largest behaviour change. 0 A.D. and OpenRA replace pair tests with per-cell counters that each unit increments over its footprint and updates as a circle difference on movement ([0 A.D. CCmpRangeManager.cpp](https://github.com/0ad/0ad/blob/master/source/simulation2/components/CCmpRangeManager.cpp); [OpenRA Shroud.cs](https://github.com/OpenRA/OpenRA/blob/bleed/OpenRA.Game/Traits/Player/Shroud.cs)), which costs O(footprint delta) instead of O(pairs) but ignores walls entirely; only Spring keeps occlusion, by raycasting each unit's footprint against the heightmap with precomputed per-radius ray tables and caching the result per instance ([Spring LosMap.cpp](https://github.com/spring/spring/blob/develop/rts/Sim/Misc/LosMap.cpp)). For bluproto a per-guild presence or danger field written once per body and read in O(1) by neighbours is a strong cheap filter (skip the pair if the target's tile is not in the observer's stamped footprint), and it extends the pheromone field that already exists, but replacing occluded LOS with it is a change to what creatures are, not an optimisation.

## The ordered plan, with expected gains and what each step changes

The table gives the recommended order, the nature of each step, the gain the evidence supports, and how to validate it. Gains are estimates from the notes' arithmetic, not measurements; the profile after each step decides whether the next is needed. The baseline extrapolation at 10,000 bodies is ~150 ms: ~56 ms of rays, ~13 ms of other sense work, and ~80 ms of think, act, census and world updates whose internal split is unknown.

| Order | Step | Exact or behaviour change | Expected effect | Validate by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Flat primitive tile arrays for opacity and connectivity; integer Amanatides-Woo traversal in `Grid.hasLOS`; dot-product cone test with per-body `StrictMath` heading; one march per unordered pair | Exact for geometry (not bit-identical at corner clips the old sampler got wrong); cone test equivalent up to boundary rounding | Ray loop iterations down 5.7-8x, tile loads from ~3 dependent to 1 resident; rays from ~56 ms toward ~8-14 ms at 10k; pair sharing up to a further 2x where ranges overlap | JMH on `hasLOS` before/after; fixture diff limited to corner-clip cases; demo world unchanged |
| 2 | Allocation-free sense: SoA output slots, counting-sort grid rebuild in id order, no per-tick lists; keep the ray kernel small and monomorphic | Exact | Not quotable from sources; removes GC pauses and cache misses, and is the prerequisite for step 4 | JFR allocation profile at zero in the tick; tick-time variance |
| 3 | Static-occluder tile-pair table (walls) as O(1) pre-filter, exact DDA only on survivors against the dynamic layer; promote to full cached table if tall-grass churn is low | Behaviour change: LOS quantised to tile centres (also buys full symmetry); deterministic if invalidation is state-keyed | Remaining ray cost toward ~2 ms at 10k; 7.5-15 MB and seconds of parallel precompute at load | Count of dynamic-occluder changes per tick; fixture re-baseline; demo world guild counts either side |
| 4 | Parallel sense (and think) over a frozen snapshot on a dedicated 4-thread pool with fixed id-range partitions; act applied serially in id order | Bit-reproducible replay, but a behaviour change if sense and act are interleaved today (sequential to synchronous update) | 3.3-3.6x on the parallel share measured locally; ~2.3x on the whole tick at today's 80% parallel share, less once steps 1-3 shrink it | 1/2/4-thread curve with act stubbed; full `SimTests` re-baseline; demo world either side |
| 5 | Sleep for bodies with no neighbours in range; hash-offset cadence tiers keyed on danger and neighbour count; fixed per-tick ray budget with round-robin carry | Behaviour change: bounded reaction latency (cap at the cadence, e.g. 4 ticks); neutral only where wake conditions are complete | ~4x on the sensing share under a 10/40/50 tier split (model); bounds worst-case cost regardless of population | Tier histogram over a demo run; latency of predator detection; guild counts either side; confirm no correlation between tier and heritable traits |
| 6 | Checkerboard act partitioning; per-guild presence and danger fields as pair filters | Behaviour change: a different fixed act order; fields drop occlusion unless Spring-style stamped | Needed only if the serial act phase exceeds ~10 ms at 10k (over ~1 µs per body) | Act-phase profile after step 4 |

The decisive measurement is the one not yet taken: the share of the tick spent in act. If act is under ~1 µs per body, steps one to four alone leave the projected 10,000-body tick in the 20-30 ms range and step five is a safety margin; if act is at or above ~3 µs per body today, 10,000 bodies means a 30 ms serial act phase and step five's sleeping (which skips act for idle bodies too) or step six's partitioning becomes mandatory. Either way the first commit is the same, and it is exact.

## Conclusion

The survey shifts the question from "which optimisation" to "which semantics are negotiable". Half the distance to 10,000 bodies is available without touching behaviour, and all of it lives in one function and one data structure: the eight-samples-per-tile march over a grid of pointers is doing the work of an exact traversal over a 50 KB byte array at several times the cost, and fixing that is the only step whose outcome is certain. The other half forces a choice that no published engine has avoided: quantise sight to tiles, let bodies see start-of-tick state, or let them sense less often. Each is a deterministic, replayable change, and each changes what a creature is, so each belongs behind the repo's own test, a demo world run either side and read by guild counts rather than by a passing scenario.

The deeper implication is that the sense pass should be redesigned as a scheduler rather than a loop. Unreal, Spring, Factorio and RimWorld all converge on persistent per-pair or per-entity records with importance, age and count-based budgets, and that shape, once built, absorbs every later scale step without further semantic change, whereas a faster loop only postpones the wall by one factor of ten. Building the exact steps first buys the profile that tells which behaviour change is cheapest, and building the scheduler second makes the number of bodies a parameter instead of a limit.
