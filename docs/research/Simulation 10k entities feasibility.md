# 10k bodies fit four cores, not two shared vCPUs

**Verdict: 10,000 creatures at 33 ticks/s is reachable on the 4-core dev box, but only by stacking three layers — exact algorithmic cuts (~40%), a parallel sense phase (~3.5x on the sense share) and behaviour-changing cadence on the act path — and it is not reachable on the live Hetzner CX22, whose single shared vCPU is ~0.43 of a desktop core and whose realistic ceiling after the whole plan is 2,000–4,000 bodies.** The code today runs 2,300 bodies in 27.9 ms (93% of budget) and ~9,600 bodies in 186 ms (6.2x over) on the dev box ([x8a.out](file:///tmp/claude-0/-home-user-bluproto/0be94016-96e2-53df-94b3-6fde065aaab2/scratchpad/prof2/x8a.out), [x40w.out](file:///tmp/claude-0/-home-user-bluproto/0be94016-96e2-53df-94b3-6fde065aaab2/scratchpad/prof2/x40w.out)); the target is 3 µs of wall time per body per tick against 19.3 µs today. The hardware comparison is the evidence for the verdict: no shipped CPU game or framework runs 10,000 individually sensing agents with per-agent occluded sight every tick — Dwarf Fortress tops out near ~500 units with LOS as its slowest subsystem, Project Zomboid pins a 9800X3D core at 2,500 zombies, and the ones that reach 10k+ (Songs of Syx, Spring/Recoil, Cities: Skylines 2) do so with no per-pair line of sight, throttled off-screen agents and job-system threading. The earlier report's ray-centred plan is obsolete: six of its steps have landed, the whole LOS stack is now 11% of the tick, and the cost that grows with population is the crowd gather (`Buckets.near`, 20% self) plus the attention loop that walks it. The decisive constraint is the serial floor — census, movement, collisions and the genuinely mutating act — which is ~16% of the tick, i.e. ~30 ms on its own at 10k bodies; parallelism cannot touch it, so the last layer has to be cadence on act, not more sense optimisation.

Three things follow. First, bluproto's per-body cost is already in Dwarf Fortress / Project Zomboid territory (12–19 µs vs ~17–20 µs), and those are the two games known for dying at hundreds to low thousands of agents. Second, every system in the 10k+ club pays for it with cheaper perception and off-screen throttling: RimWorld's RocketMan mod takes animals from 60 Hz to 5 Hz for ~+150 TPS ([Skymods RocketMan](https://catalogue.smods.ru/archives/78225)), Unreal's sight sense performs at most 6 traces per tick under an age+importance queue ([AISense_Sight.cpp](https://github.com/JimmieKJ/unrealTournament/blob/master/Engine/Source/Runtime/AIModule/Private/Perception/AISense_Sight.cpp)), Spring's staggered mode revisits each unit every 15 frames ([LosHandler.cpp](https://github.com/spring/spring/blob/develop/rts/Sim/Misc/LosHandler.cpp)). Third, the Java runtime is not the limit: MASON runs the large flocking benchmark at **0.61x the time of compiled Agents.jl** ([ABMFrameworksComparison](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/JuliaDynamics/ABMFrameworksComparison/main/README.md)), and Dominion sweeps entities at 0.7–3.9 ns each ([Dominion benchmarks](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/dominion-dev/dominion-ecs-java/main/dominion-ecs-engine-benchmarks/OTHERS.md)), so iteration overhead is noise; the microseconds are in what each body does per tick.

The budget arithmetic is unforgiving. One CX22 vCPU for the sim, minus a 20–40% allowance for the measured run-to-run variance and for G1 pauses, WebSocket serialisation and Caddy on the same two vCPUs, leaves **~18–24 ms of CX22 time per tick, ≈ 9–16 ms of dev-box time**. Anything over ~12 ms on the dev box is likely to overrun live under neighbour load. Today's 27.9 ms at 2,300 bodies therefore maps to ~42–56 ms live: the live ceiling with current code is **~1,000–1,500 bodies**, not 2,300. Moving to dedicated vCPUs is the only hardware route to 10k live: CCX23 (4 dedicated EPYC Milan threads, 16 GB) is €85.99/mo after the 15 June 2026 repricing, +€80/mo over CX23, and its per-thread speed (~1.85x a CX22 vCPU) is roughly the dev box's ([wz-it.com](https://wz-it.com/en/blog/hetzner-price-increase-june-2026-cpx-ccx-alternatives/), [sparecores ccx23](https://sparecores.com/server/hcloud/ccx23)). An existing CX22 keeps its old price; migrating forfeits it.

The Amdahl ceiling settles what parallelism can do. With sense at 67.1%, perfect 4-way parallel sense turns 186 ms into **92 ms**; infinite cores leave the serial 61 ms. Parallelising every scan (84%) leaves a **~30 ms serial floor at 10k bodies — the whole budget** ([phases.txt](file:///tmp/claude-0/-home-user-bluproto/0be94016-96e2-53df-94b3-6fde065aaab2/scratchpad/prof2/phases.txt)). The earlier report's "decisive unknown" is answered: broad act is 2.5–3.6 µs per body, but half of it is perception in disguise (`scanTargets` 1.8 µs, `nearestMate` 1.1 µs); the genuinely mutating part is ~0.8 µs per body, which is what makes the plan below possible.

Steps 1–4 are the first commits regardless of verdict: they are exact, they remove the density term that makes the curve superlinear, and together they bring the 10k tick to roughly the per-body rate the code already achieves at 3,300 bodies. Step 1 is the largest single win in the profile and the cheapest; the symmetric cell walk is also what BioDynaMo found decisive before its 65x scaling ([arXiv 2301.06984](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.06984)). Step 2 may be the cheapest exact 10% in the codebase or may be a behaviour change, and one grep decides which.

Step 5 is where the dev box and the live box part ways. The local fork-join harness measured **3.3–3.6x on four fixed partitions** for 0.2–2 µs of per-body work ([FjBench.java](file:///tmp/claude-0/-home-user-bluproto/0be94016-96e2-53df-94b3-6fde065aaab2/scratchpad/FjBench.java)), and bluproto's sense is compute-heavy enough per byte to avoid Factorio's memory-bound outcome, where threading a "read two variables, add" update cut 0.5 ms to 0.39 ms at 30x the CPU ([FFF-421](https://www.factorio.com/blog/post/fff-421), [FFF-215](https://www.factorio.com/blog/post/fff-215)). On two shared vCPUs the same step is worth perhaps 1.5x, and only when the second vCPU is not serving viewers. The semantic cost is real: every body sees start-of-tick state, and the canonical result in evolutionary games is that synchronous versus asynchronous update can flip a population's outcome ([arXiv 0911.1720](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0911.1720)); it is the pattern every deterministic engine uses (Unity sort-keyed command buffers, Factorio's merged wake lists, [entity-command-buffers.md](https://github.com/Unity-Technologies/EntityComponentSystemSamples/blob/master/EntitiesSamples/Docs/entity-command-buffers.md), [FFF-364](https://factorio.com/blog/post/fff-364)), but it is a behaviour change and has to be read on guild counts.

Step 6 is the one that reaches 30 ms, and it is the industry's answer rather than an optimisation: RimWorld 1.6 ticks things as slow as 4 Hz, RocketMan throttles animals 60 → 5 Hz for ~+150 TPS, Minecraft runs mob AI only within 32 blocks ([Ludeon 1.6 blog](https://ludeon.com/blog/2025/06/announcing-odyssey-and-update-1-6/), [Skymods RocketMan](https://catalogue.smods.ru/archives/78225), [space-node](https://space-node.net/blog/minecraft-entity-activation-range-mob-caps-2026)). Bluproto already has the sense clock; what is missing is applying it to think and act, and sleeping bodies with nothing in range, which is behaviour-neutral only if the wake condition (a body entering the cell neighbourhood) is complete. Schedules stay deterministic as long as they are pure functions of (tick, id, state) — counts, never wall-clock slices ([HugsLib tick scheduling](https://github.com/UnlimitedHugs/RimworldHugsLib/wiki/Custom-Tick-Scheduling)) — and the repo's own balance rule adds a constraint no game has: tiers must derive from situation, not from heritable traits, or the scheduler becomes a selective force.

The population crash is the finding that reframes the question. At x32 and x40 the harness loses 11–13% of seeded bodies before tick 100 and a further 35% by tick 600; "10k entities" on the 288x176 demo map is a transient at one body per five tiles, and the superlinear cost is the same density. A larger map would both carry the population ecologically and remove the density term — the x16 rate of 11.8 µs per body at 10k bodies is 118 ms before any step, against 186 ms measured — so map size is a performance knob as much as a balance one.

## Conclusion

The question has changed shape since September. Then it was "how to make rays cheap"; the memo answered that, and what is left is a shape problem: a gather that copies the crowd several times per body, a legacy perception pass nobody may read, and a serial act phase that is already the whole budget at 10k. The exact steps are now about data movement rather than geometry, and they are worth ~40% on their own. The behaviour-changing steps are the same ones every 10k-agent game shipped, and bluproto has already taken the first of them (the sense clock) without the sky falling; extending that clock to think and act is the step that reaches 30 ms, and the one that most needs the guild-count test.

The hardware comparison gives the honest framing for the live world. A CX22 vCPU is a 2008–2012 desktop core with a noisy neighbour; the systems that run 10k agents recommend 4 GHz quad-cores and X3D caches and still throttle. Ten thousand bodies live is therefore a hardware decision (+€80/mo for CCX23, which is roughly the dev box) layered on the software plan, not a software plan alone — and the software plan's exact half should land first either way, because it is the only part whose outcome is certain and it buys the profile that prices the rest.
